The problem

Pinnacle AI, where I work, builds automation for businesses. Our client runs 10 coffee shops. Each store manager handles staff schedules, ordering, inventory and food waste on top of running the store, so the goal was to take repetitive work off their plate.

Schedules were the hardest to keep up. Managers tried to reuse a familiar weekly roster, but people's availability kept changing. Each change meant collecting updates, reassigning shifts and contacting replacements, and each unanswered message left something unresolved.

How might we cut the back-and-forth of keeping a schedule working, while keeping managers in charge of the decisions that need them?

What I found

I started with the work itself rather than the request. We surveyed all 10 managers, I interviewed four, and I shadowed one through a working day to see how they actually ran their team.

Four repetitive tasks came up, each taking about 2 hours a week per store. At a loaded manager cost of $23/hr, that adds up to about $95.7K a year across 10 stores: a projection, used to decide what was worth automating.

Scheduling had a pattern the others didn't. Generating a roster was only part of it; most of the work was the coordination that kept the roster workable. So the product had to take on the conversations, not just the schedule.

Five design decisions

Each decision traded something away. Here's what we chose, and why.

Decision 1

Start with scheduling, not ordering

  • Ordering and inventory firstEasier to automate: data-based and already on a computer. Not what managers most wanted help with.
  • Scheduling firstHarder: people, changing availability, exceptions. But managers wanted to hand it off and trust an agent with it.

My prioritization weighed technical feasibility against willingness to adopt the result. We accepted more interaction complexity for a workflow managers wanted help with sooner, and it gave a clear test of value: how much scheduling work stays with the manager once the agent is involved?

Decision 2

Work in iMessage, not a new app

  • Standalone appA dense roster is easier to inspect, but needs installs, accounts and learning first.
  • iMessageEveryone already has it open. The conversation design has to carry all the clarity.

Each employee has a private conversation with the agent, and the manager has a separate thread for updates, approvals and corrections. A manager can change the schedule just by messaging the agent. Each part is built so it can later merge into one platform.

Decision 3

Propose a schedule instead of asking questions

  • Ask one question at a time~10 messages per person, and employees kept getting pulled back in.
  • Propose, then confirm3 messages: the agent proposes, the employee responds, the agent confirms. The cost is a denser first message.

The first flow asked employees when they could work, built a schedule, then kept coming back whenever something didn't fit: could they adjust their availability, work more hours, or fewer? I redesigned the exchange around a recommendation, so the employee responds to something concrete. No model change: the win was the question flow. Unusual situations can still need clarification.

Decision 4

Ask the manager only when something is new

  • Ask about everythingSafe, but the manager still does the job.
  • Never askFastest, but new situations get decided without anyone's judgment.
  • Ask only when it's newRoutine work runs itself; the manager is interrupted only when their judgment matters.

A delayed flight put this to the test. An employee would miss two morning shifts, and nobody at their store could cover. The agent looked to a nearby store, moved past an unavailable first candidate, found someone who could help, and asked the manager to approve before updating the schedule. The manager made the decision; the agent did the search, the messages and the update.

Silence is handled the same way: if an employee doesn't answer a check-in, the agent follows up after 6 hours, then asks the manager. One case doesn't prove every exception works, though. Call-outs and cross-store changes are still harder than routine scheduling.

Decision 5

Turn a manager's correction into a rule

  • Fix it each timeSimple, but the manager fixes the same mistake every week.
  • Save it as a ruleThe manager corrects it once by message, and the agent can use it again.

A busy morning needed four experienced people, but the schedule had three plus a new hire. The headcount was right; the mix was wrong. The same mix would have been fine on a quiet day.

The manager set the rule by message: on busy days (Fridays, good weather, festivals, nearby school events), mornings need people with six months or more. A strict rule every day would cut flexibility for no reason; ignoring it on busy days leaves a store underprepared. The agent saves corrections like this as rules. Whether it applies them consistently is still being evaluated.

Why it needs AI

A roster alone doesn't need AI: fixed scheduling rules work in ordinary software. What needs AI is the conversation around it. People describe availability in their own words, decline, and change plans, so the next useful step depends on what was just said.

Today, AI reads replies, extracts availability, builds schedules and decides whom to contact. My part was designing how people take part: what employees are asked, what managers need to know, and where a person decides. The developer built the model integration, messaging and backend.

Results

~2–3 hoursweekly scheduling work per manager per store before (managers' estimates)
~5–10 minutesafter (totaled afterward, averaged over a month)

The comparison covers all 10 stores, including routine scheduling, call-outs and cross-store changes. What's left is mostly approving new situations and improving the rules by message. Both figures rely on recall, so this is an operational comparison, not a controlled study.

Accuracy over time

Accuracy means how rarely the manager has to correct or rebuild the schedule. It started at 20–30%, rose about 5 points a month, dropped about 20 points during three months of frequent call-outs, then recovered.

Trust followed that curve. At first the manager was skeptical and frustrated: it felt like training the system while still doing the job. Now they no longer build schedules by hand.

What's next

Inventory management is live too, with managers approving its output until it's accurate enough to run on its own. Waste management is next. Each agent moves up the same ladder as manager intervention drops.

We're also testing points or recognition for unpopular shifts, so they get picked up without the manager chasing people. And next time I'd measure more finely, separating wrong outputs from fair approval requests and from changes caused by new availability, since each needs a different fix.

What I learned

My most useful decisions came from looking closely at the work people were doing:

  • Look at the work, not the request. Shadowing showed that coordination, not roster-building, was the real job.
  • Fix the conversation before the model. Going from 10 messages to 3 needed no model change.
  • Design the hand-off. The agent should finish what it can and bring people only the decisions that need them.

That's what I want to keep designing toward: managers contributing their judgment without having to hold every conversation needed to act on it.

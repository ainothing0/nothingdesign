The problem

Pinnacle AI, where I work, builds automation for businesses. Our client runs 10 coffee shops. Each store manager handles staff schedules, ordering, inventory and food waste on top of running the store.

Schedules were the hardest to keep up. Managers reused a familiar weekly roster, but availability kept changing, and each change meant collecting updates, reassigning shifts and contacting replacements.

How might we cut the back-and-forth of keeping a schedule working, while keeping managers in charge of the decisions that need them?

I started with the work itself rather than the request. We surveyed all 10 managers, I interviewed four, and I shadowed one through a working day.

Four repetitive tasks came up, each about 2 hours a week per store. At a loaded manager cost of $23/hr that is about $95.7K a year across 10 stores: a projection, used to decide what was worth automating.

Scheduling had a pattern the others didn't: most of the work was the coordination that kept the roster workable. So the product had to take on the conversations, not just the schedule.

Five design decisions

Decision 1

Start with scheduling, not ordering

  • Ordering and inventory firstEasier to automate: data-based and already on a computer. Not what managers most wanted help with.
  • Scheduling firstHarder: people, changing availability, exceptions. But managers wanted to hand it off and trust an agent with it.

We accepted more interaction complexity for a workflow managers wanted to hand off. It also gave a clear test of value: how much scheduling work stays with the manager once the agent is involved?

Decision 2

Work in iMessage, not a new app

  • Standalone appA dense roster is easier to inspect, but needs installs, accounts and learning first.
  • iMessageEveryone already has it open. The conversation design has to carry all the clarity.

Each employee has a private thread with the agent. The manager has a separate one for updates, approvals and corrections, and can change the schedule just by messaging. Each part is built so it can later merge into one platform.

Decision 3

Propose a schedule instead of asking questions

  • Ask one question at a time~10 messages per person, and employees kept getting pulled back in.
  • Propose, then confirm3 messages: the agent proposes, the employee responds, the agent confirms. The cost is a denser first message.

I redesigned the exchange around a recommendation, so the employee responds to something concrete. No model change: the win was the question flow. Unusual situations can still need clarification.

Decision 4

Ask the manager only when something is new

  • Ask about everythingSafe, but the manager still does the job.
  • Never askFastest, but new situations get decided without anyone's judgment.
  • Ask only when it's newRoutine work runs itself; the manager is interrupted only when their judgment matters.

A delayed flight tested this. An employee would miss two morning shifts and nobody at their store could cover. The agent looked to a nearby store, moved past an unavailable first candidate, found someone, and asked the manager to approve before updating the schedule. Silence works the same way: no reply to a check-in gets a follow-up after 6 hours, then goes to the manager.

One case doesn't prove every exception works. Call-outs and cross-store changes are still harder than routine scheduling.

Decision 5

Turn a manager's correction into a rule

  • Fix it each timeSimple, but the manager fixes the same mistake every week.
  • Save it as a ruleThe manager corrects it once by message, and the agent can use it again.

A busy morning needed four experienced people, but the schedule had three plus a new hire. The manager set the rule by message: on busy days (Fridays, good weather, festivals, nearby school events), mornings need people with six months or more. The agent saves corrections like this as rules. Whether it applies them consistently is still being evaluated.

Why it needs AI

A roster alone doesn't need AI: fixed rules work in ordinary software. What needs AI is the conversation around it, because people describe availability in their own words, decline, and change plans. AI reads replies, extracts availability, builds schedules and decides whom to contact. I designed how people take part: what employees are asked, what managers need to know, and where a person decides.

What's next

Inventory management is live too, with managers approving its output until it's accurate enough to run on its own. Waste management is next. Each agent moves up the same ladder as manager intervention drops.

We're also testing points or recognition for unpopular shifts. And next time I'd measure more finely, separating wrong outputs from fair approval requests and from changes caused by new availability, since each needs a different fix.

What I learned

  • Look at the work, not the request. Shadowing showed that coordination, not roster-building, was the real job.
  • Fix the conversation before the model. Going from 10 messages to 3 needed no model change.
  • Design the hand-off. The agent should finish what it can and bring people only the decisions that need them.

That's what I want to keep designing toward: managers contributing their judgment without having to hold every conversation needed to act on it.

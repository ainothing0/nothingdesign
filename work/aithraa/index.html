Aithraa — Phase 1 Case study

Aithraa Phase 1: building a 3-sided marketplace until IP rights made it unbuildable

Product Designer & Founder · February 2025 – June 2025

In my last semester of a master's in HCI, I noticed a pattern: every university runs capstone courses where outside clients bring real projects for students to build — but matching the right client to the right course was ad hoc, dependent on whichever professor happened to have a connection in their own network, not on actual fit with student talent. I designed a 3-sided marketplace — Where Startups Meet Student Builders — connecting startups, students, and professors directly, on fit instead of convenience. Starting as a 4-person, all-product-designer capstone team and then continuing solo for ~6 months, I validated the idea through 25+ interviews and a working Figma prototype. I lined up collaboration agreements with 3 universities (CMU, IU, Purdue) set to start the following January semester, and onboarded 3 startup founders as clients. The matching product never launched: once real collaboration was underway, a non-negotiable IP-rights conflict surfaced — universities claim ownership of student work, startups paying for it need clean IP — and no workaround landed. I pivoted to Phase 2 (demolition cost estimating — see the Estbuild case study) only after shutting this down.

How it started

I'd been circling pieces of this problem across earlier work, and they clicked together in my last semester of HCI grad school: universities already run capstone courses where startups and other outside clients bring in real projects for students to build. The matching, though, happened informally — through whichever professor had the right connection, not through anything designed for the job. The idea: Where Startups Meet Student Builders — a platform that does that matching directly, instead of leaving it to chance.

I partly explored this with a professor as a potential partner, but that didn't work out over differences — so I kept building it on my own.

The three sides, and what each one actually wanted

  • Startups wanted to test an idea's feasibility with a cheap MVP — not necessarily production-grade work, but enough to validate direction.
  • Students wanted a real-world project and industry experience — not a dummy exercise, something with actual impact on their portfolio and skills.
  • Professors wanted projects that matched their curriculum and gave students genuine industry exposure, while filling the gatekeeper/matchmaker role capstone courses already needed someone to play.

The actual misalignment: professors sourced capstone clients from their own existing network by default — not matched for fit with the students' actual talent or skills. That default optimized for professor convenience, not for startup or student outcomes, and it's why student work often didn't actually help the startup client: the match itself was frequently wrong before any work even started. Aithraa's core pitch was replacing that network-of-convenience matching with matching on goals, skills, and timeline.

Team and my role

I started this inside a 4-person team — all product designers — during a capstone course. After that course ended, I continued alone for about 6 months, operating as a solo founder before pivoting to Estbuild.

What I owned: I identified the problem and then built the platform itself — UI/UX, the matching logic, and the onboarding flow — personally, in Figma.

Designing the solution

The onboarding flow, as designed:

  1. Submit details — tell us about your startup or course, a few questions to get started.
  2. Get matched — paired with the right course based on goals, skills, and timeline.
  3. Discuss your vision — talk with the professor and shape the collaboration roadmap together.
  4. Collaboration & build — turn the concept into reality through hands-on work with student builders.

The original plan was to run the matching itself through a conversational agent over message and email, then move users into a dashboard platform once matched — chat first, structured UI second, a pattern I'd return to deliberately on the Pinnacle AI scheduling agent.

I validated the concept through 25+ user interviews using contextual immersion and formal research, built a unified platform experience in Figma, and iterated functional prototypes against continuous user feedback — a build-test-learn loop adapted for AI-era development speed. The matched flow itself never went live on real projects — see Traction, below.

Landing 3 university partnerships

I landed CMU, Indiana University, and Purdue through direct outreach — visiting Pittsburgh, attending events, working personal connections — eventually connecting with a CMU HCI course client manager who was already doing the same kind of matching work manually. That relationship was the door-opener into CMU specifically. All 3 partnerships were collaboration agreements set to begin the following January semester — CMU specifically came in as a university course/professor relationship, not as a startup-side client.

Why it died: the IP-rights wall

The constraint: university policy claims ownership of student work; startups — who are paying for the work — need clean IP to use it. Both positions were non-negotiable, on both sides.

This didn't show up as a day-one dealbreaker. It surfaced only once real collaboration with the universities was underway — partnerships committed, startup clients onboarded — not during early exploratory conversations, and not only after a full cohort had run a project to completion. It hit before the matching product itself ever launched.

I tried to find a workaround — specifically, a path for students to transfer IP to the startup — but that needed buy-in from a key stakeholder that never materialized. Being constrained on that core piece of the vision (clean IP transfer, non-negotiable for founders) is what forced the pivot.

The Phase 2 idea (AI-assisted demolition cost estimating, a technical co-founder, the "Estbuild" angle) wasn't already in motion as a backup plan — it only surfaced after Phase 1 was shut down.

What I'd carry forward

  • Treat IP/ownership terms as a go/no-go gate before onboarding a multi-party pilot into real operation — not after the product is already working and users are already in it.
  • A validated 3-sided match doesn't protect you from a structural legal constraint that only one side can waive. Demand and legal feasibility are separate gates, and both have to clear.
  • Chat/conversational matching before a structured dashboard was the right early instinct for onboarding — a pattern worth carrying forward deliberately rather than rediscovering each time.

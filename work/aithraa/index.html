Aithraa Phase 1: building a 3‑sided marketplace until IP rights made it unbuildable

A marketplace matching startups, students and professors on fit, validated on all three sides and stopped by an IP-rights conflict before launch.

The problem

In my last semester of a master's in HCI, I noticed a pattern: universities run capstone courses where outside clients bring real projects for students to build, but the matching happened informally, through whichever professor had the right connection.

Professors sourced clients from their own network by default, not for fit with the students' skills. That is why student work often didn't help the startup: the match was wrong before any work started. Aithraa's pitch was to replace matching by convenience with matching on goals, skills and timeline.

Three sides, and what each wanted

Side 1Startups

Test an idea's feasibility with a cheap MVP, enough to validate direction

Side 2Students

A real project and industry experience, not a dummy exercise

Side 3Professors

Projects that fit the curriculum and give students real industry exposure

My role

I started this inside a 4-person team of product designers during a capstone course, then continued alone as a solo founder. I identified the problem and built the platform in Figma myself: UI/UX, the matching logic and the onboarding flow. I partly explored it with a professor as a potential partner; that didn't work out, so I kept building on my own.

The design

The plan was to run the matching through a conversational agent over message and email, then move people into a dashboard once matched: chat first, structured UI second. I returned to that pattern deliberately on the Pinnacle AI scheduling agent.

I validated the concept through 25+ interviews, using contextual immersion and formal research, and iterated the Figma prototype against user feedback. The matched flow never went live on real projects.

Traction

I landed CMU, Indiana University and Purdue through direct outreach: visiting Pittsburgh, attending events, working personal connections. A CMU HCI course client manager, who was already doing this matching by hand, opened the door there. All three were collaboration agreements set to begin the following January semester, and three startup founders were onboarded as clients.

The traction was commitments, not usage. No match ever ran.

Why it stopped: the IP-rights wall

It didn't show up on day one. It surfaced once real collaboration was underway, with partnerships committed and founders onboarded, and before the product launched. I looked for a workaround, a path for students to transfer IP to the startup, but it needed buy-in from a key stakeholder that never came.

So I shut it down. Estbuild, the second phase, was not a backup plan already in motion; it came afterwards.

What I learned

  • Treat IP and ownership terms as a go/no-go gate before bringing a multi-party pilot into real operation.
  • Demand and legal feasibility are separate gates. A validated three-sided match doesn't protect you from a constraint only one side can waive.
  • Chat before dashboard was the right instinct for onboarding, and worth carrying forward on purpose.

What this project shows

Systems thinking
I mapped what three sides each wanted and found the failure in how they were matched, not in any one of them.
